Q1

Define food irradiation and name the three main sources of ionizing radiation.

Food irradiation is the treatment of food with a controlled dose of ionizing radiation. It can reduce selected microorganisms, insects and sprouting. The main sources are gamma rays, electron beams and X-rays. Treatment is performed under controlled conditions. Example: irradiation of spices to reduce microbial contamination.

Q2

Explain the principle of food irradiation.

Ionizing radiation interacts with food and damages the genetic material of susceptible microorganisms. This can prevent them from multiplying. It may also control insects, parasites or sprouting, depending on the dose and product. The treatment can be performed with relatively little temperature increase. Example: irradiating potatoes to inhibit sprouting.

Q3

List four applications of food irradiation.

Irradiation can reduce selected pathogens, control insects in cereals and pulses, inhibit sprouting in potatoes and onions, and treat selected meat or fish products for parasite control. The suitable dose depends on the target and food. It can also improve the microbial quality of spices. Example: treating spices before packaging.

Q4

State two advantages and two limitations of food irradiation.

Advantages include reducing selected microbial hazards and extending shelf life. It can treat some foods without conventional high-temperature heating. Limitations include that it cannot restore spoiled food or remove every toxin and chemical contaminant. Some foods may undergo sensory or nutrient changes. Example: spice irradiation improves microbial quality but does not replace hygiene.

Q5

Does food become radioactive after approved irradiation? Mention two possible quality effects.

No, food does not become radioactive simply because it has undergone an approved irradiation process. Irradiation may reduce microorganisms and extend shelf life. Depending on dose and food type, it can change flavour, texture or colour. Some vitamins may also be reduced. Example: irradiation may lower the microbial load of spices.

Q6

Define food concentration and state two reasons for concentrating liquid foods.

Food concentration removes part of the water from a liquid food, increasing the proportion of solids. It reduces product volume and weight. This can lower storage and transport costs. Concentration can also prepare products for further processing such as drying. Example: concentrating fruit juice before powder production.

Q7

What is solar concentration? Give one advantage and one limitation.

Solar concentration uses energy from sunlight to evaporate water from a food solution. Its advantage is that it uses a renewable energy source and may reduce fuel use. Its limitation is dependence on sunlight and weather. Process control can be less consistent than with engineered evaporators. Example: solar evaporation of salt solutions.

Q8

Differentiate between open-kettle evaporation and flash evaporation.

Open-kettle evaporation heats liquid food in an open vessel. Flash evaporation rapidly vaporizes part of a heated liquid when its pressure is reduced. Open-kettle systems are simple but may involve longer heat exposure. Flash evaporation is rapid under suitable conditions. Example: syrup cooking versus flash concentration of a liquid food.

Q9

What is a thin-film evaporator, and why is it suitable for heat-sensitive foods?

A thin-film evaporator spreads liquid food as a thin layer over a heated surface. The thin layer supports rapid heat transfer and evaporation. Short residence time can reduce heat-related quality damage. It is useful when prolonged heating would harm flavour or nutrients. Example: concentrating selected fruit juices.

Q10

What is vacuum evaporation, and how does reduced pressure help?

Vacuum evaporation concentrates liquid food at a pressure below atmospheric pressure. Reduced pressure lowers the boiling temperature of water. Evaporation can therefore occur at a lower temperature than at atmospheric pressure. This can help protect heat-sensitive quality attributes. Example: concentrating fruit juice under vacuum.

Q11

Define freeze concentration and state two advantages.

Freeze concentration removes water by forming ice crystals and separating them from the remaining liquid. It can limit exposure to high temperatures. It may retain fresh flavour compounds better than prolonged evaporation. The process requires effective ice separation. Example: concentrating fruit juice while preserving aroma.

Q12

Differentiate between ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis.

Ultrafiltration separates components mainly by membrane pore size and molecular size, retaining many proteins while allowing water and smaller solutes through. Reverse osmosis uses a dense membrane and pressure to separate water from dissolved substances. RO is commonly used for water removal; UF is useful for fractionating macromolecules. Example: UF of whey proteins and RO concentration of liquid foods.

Q13

Define permeate and retentate.

Permeate is the portion that passes through a membrane. Retentate is the portion retained on the feed side. Their composition depends on membrane properties and operating conditions. Membranes can concentrate or fractionate food components. Example: protein-rich whey retentate during ultrafiltration.

Q14

How do sugar and salt help preserve foods?

Sugar and salt reduce water activity by making water less available for microbial growth. This can limit the growth of many microorganisms. Sugar is commonly used in jams and syrups. Salt is used in pickles and cured meat. Example: high sugar concentration helps preserve jam.

Q15

Define water activity and write its relationship with equilibrium relative humidity.

Water activity measures the availability of water for microbial growth and reactions. It is the ratio of the food's water-vapour pressure to that of pure water at the same temperature: aw = p/p⁰. At equilibrium, ERH (%) is approximately aw × 100. Example: aw 0.70 corresponds to about 70% ERH.

Q16

Differentiate between moisture content and water activity.

Moisture content is the total amount of water in food. Water activity describes how available that water is for microbial growth and chemical reactions. Foods with similar moisture contents can have different water activities. Water activity is often more useful for predicting microbial stability. Example: jam and fresh fruit can have different water activities.

Q17

Differentiate between free water and bound water.

Free water is relatively available for microbial growth and reactions. Bound water is associated with food components and is less available. Their relative proportions affect food stability. Total water includes both free and bound water. Example: water associated with proteins is less available than freely mobile water.

Q18

State the approximate water-activity limits for most bacteria and most yeasts and moulds in the notes.

The lecture notes state that most bacteria cannot grow below aw 0.85. Most yeasts and moulds cannot grow below aw 0.65. These are approximate general limits, not universal limits for every species. Some microorganisms tolerate lower water activity. Example: reducing aw helps stabilise dried foods.

Q19

Define food drying and state two purposes.

Food drying removes moisture under controlled conditions. It extends shelf life by reducing water activity. It limits microbial growth and many enzyme activities. It also reduces food weight and storage or transport costs. Example: drying grapes to make raisins.

Q20

Mention four mechanisms of moisture movement during drying.

Capillary movement draws free water through small channels. Solid shrinkage may squeeze water towards the surface. Solid diffusion moves water through the solid due to concentration gradients. Surface diffusion moves adsorbed water along a surface. Example: moisture moves from the interior of fruit slices towards the surface.

Q21

What three air conditions are important for effective hot-air drying?

A moderately high dry-bulb temperature supplies energy for evaporation. Low relative humidity helps air accept more water vapour. High air velocity improves heat transfer and carries vapour away from the food surface. Together, these conditions can increase drying rate. Example: heated moving air drying sliced vegetables.

Q22

List four factors affecting food drying rate.

Important factors include air temperature, relative humidity, air velocity and food characteristics such as thickness and structure. Dryer design and product loading also affect performance. Higher suitable temperatures and faster air movement generally speed drying. Very harsh conditions can damage quality. Example: thin apple slices dry faster than thick slices.

Q23

How do drying and concentration reduce water activity?

Drying removes moisture from food, while concentration removes part of the water from liquid foods. Both increase the proportion of solids in the remaining product. Lower water availability can restrict microbial growth. The effect depends on the food composition and process. Example: raisins from drying and juice concentrate from evaporation.

Q24

Why are moisture-resistant packaging and proper storage important for dried foods?

Dried foods can absorb moisture from humid surroundings. This may raise water activity and allow deterioration. Suitable packaging limits moisture entry and contamination. Correct storage helps preserve texture and shelf life. Example: biscuits in a poorly sealed packet can become soft.

Q25

Name and define the three modes of heat transfer.

Conduction transfers heat through a material by molecular interactions. Convection transfers heat between a surface and a moving fluid. Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves. Heat naturally moves from higher to lower temperature. Example: a pan conducts, boiling water convects and a grill radiates heat.

Q26

State Fourier's law of heat conduction.

Fourier's law describes heat conduction caused by a temperature gradient. The equation is q = −kA(dT/dx). Here q is heat-transfer rate, k is thermal conductivity and A is area. The negative sign indicates heat flows down the temperature gradient. Example: heat passes through bread crust during baking.

Q27

Write the equation for convective heat transfer and explain its terms.

The equation is q = hA(Tf − Ts). Here q is heat-transfer rate, h is the convective heat-transfer coefficient and A is surface area. Tf is fluid temperature and Ts is surface temperature. The rate depends on area, temperature difference and h. Example: hot air heating food in an oven.

Q28

Differentiate between natural and forced convection.

Natural convection is driven by fluid-density differences caused by temperature changes. Forced convection uses a fan, pump or other device to move fluid. Forced convection gives more control over fluid movement. Both transfer heat between a surface and a fluid. Example: heating water in a pot versus fan-assisted oven heating.

Q29

Give one example each of conduction, convection and radiation in food processing.

Conduction occurs when heat passes through a metal pan into food. Convection occurs when moving hot air heats food in an oven. Radiation occurs when infrared energy heats a food surface. More than one mode may operate simultaneously. Example: grilling involves radiation, convection and contact conduction.

Q30

Why are heat and moisture transfer important during food drying?

Heat transfer supplies the energy needed to evaporate water. Moisture transfer moves water from inside the food to its surface and then into the air. Drying depends on both energy supply and water removal. Poor transfer reduces drying efficiency. Example: hot air heats vegetables and carries away water vapour.

Q31

Define food fermentation and name the major groups of microorganisms involved.

Fermentation is the transformation of food components by microorganisms or their enzymes. Major groups include bacteria, yeasts and moulds. They can produce acids, alcohols, gases and flavour compounds. Fermentation can improve preservation and product characteristics. Example: lactic acid bacteria ferment cabbage into sauerkraut.

Q32

What are lactic acid bacteria and what is their role?

Lactic acid bacteria are microorganisms that produce lactic acid from fermentable carbohydrates. They lower pH and can inhibit many undesirable microorganisms. They also contribute to flavour and texture. They are important in fermented dairy foods and vegetables. Example: LAB ferment cabbage during sauerkraut production.

Q33

State two nutritional benefits and two preservation benefits of fermentation.

Fermentation can improve digestibility and reduce some antinutritional factors. It may increase the availability of certain nutrients. Acids and other fermentation products can inhibit undesirable microorganisms. Proper fermentation can extend shelf life. Example: fermented vegetables develop acidity that helps preservation.

Q34

How do bacteria, yeasts and moulds contribute to fermented-food quality?

Bacteria may produce acids and flavour compounds. Yeasts can produce alcohol, carbon dioxide and aroma compounds. Moulds can produce enzymes that break down proteins and carbohydrates in selected foods. These activities influence flavour, texture and appearance. Example: yeast produces carbon dioxide during bread fermentation.

Q35

Mention two factors affecting fermentation and two risks of poorly controlled fermentation.

Temperature and salt concentration affect microbial activity; acidity, time and hygiene also matter. Poor control may permit undesirable microbial growth. It can cause spoilage or harmful compound formation in some foods. Process monitoring helps maintain quality and safety. Example: temperature abuse can increase histamine risk in fish products.

Q36

What is food-waste valorization, and how can fermentation contribute?

Food-waste valorization converts food-processing residues into useful products. Microorganisms can transform suitable by-products during fermentation. Products may include organic acids, enzymes or biomass. This can reduce waste and improve resource use. Example: fruit residues can be fermented to produce useful organic acids.

Q37

Define pickling and differentiate fermented and unfermented pickles.

Pickling preserves food using salt, acid or related controlled conditions. Fermented pickles rely on microbial activity to produce acids and other compounds. Unfermented pickles rely mainly on added acid, salt or other controlled conditions. Both can extend shelf life when properly processed. Example: fermented cucumbers versus vinegar-pickled cucumbers.

Q38

What are salt-stock pickling and vinegar pickling?

Salt-stock pickling uses a concentrated salt solution to preserve vegetables, often during storage or fermentation. Salt influences water availability and microbial growth. Vinegar pickling uses vinegar to provide acidity. Acidity inhibits many undesirable microorganisms. Example: vegetables stored in brine versus onions preserved in vinegar.

Q39

Explain the roles of salt, vinegar and acidity in pickle preservation.

Salt reduces water availability and influences microbial growth. Vinegar supplies acetic acid and lowers pH. Acidity inhibits many spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms. Correct salt and acid levels support safety, flavour and texture. Example: vinegar-pickled onions are preserved partly by their acidic environment.

Q40

Name two microbial groups associated with traditional pickles and explain their importance.

Lactic acid bacteria are important in many fermented pickles. Yeasts may also occur in pickle microbial communities. LAB produce acids that support preservation and flavour. Some yeasts can affect flavour but may cause spoilage under unsuitable conditions. Example: LAB produce lactic acid during cucumber fermentation.

Q41

State two benefits and two safety concerns associated with fermented pickles.

Fermentation can produce desirable flavours and improve keeping quality. It may improve availability of certain nutrients. Poor hygiene or unsuitable salt and acidity may permit undesirable growth. Improper storage can cause spoilage. Example: improperly acidified pickles may be unsafe.

Q42

Name four traditional meat-preservation methods and explain one.

Methods include drying, curing, smoking and fermentation; pickling and preservation in fat are also used. Drying removes moisture and lowers water activity. Curing uses salt and sometimes other curing ingredients to inhibit spoilage. Each method requires appropriate controls. Example: beef jerky is preserved partly by moisture removal.

Q43

Differentiate between dry curing and brine curing.

Dry curing applies salt and other suitable curing ingredients directly to meat. Brine curing treats meat with a salt-containing liquid. Both help preserve meat by controlling water availability and microbial activity. Penetration depends on meat size and processing conditions. Example: salt-rubbed ham versus brine-cured meat.

Q44

What is meat smoking? State two effects.

Meat smoking exposes meat to smoke from suitable wood or another approved source. Smoke contributes flavour and colour. Some smoke compounds can inhibit certain microorganisms and oxidation. Smoking must be combined with proper temperature control and hygiene. Example: smoked sausage develops a characteristic aroma.

Q45

Differentiate between jerky and biltong.

Jerky is dried meat commonly prepared as thin strips. Biltong is a traditional dried meat product associated with Southern Africa. Both often use salt and seasonings. Recipes, drying conditions and textures can differ. Example: seasoned dried beef strips may be sold as jerky or biltong.

Q46

What are rillettes, and how does fat coverage contribute to preservation?

Rillettes are made by slowly cooking meat in fat until tender and shreddable. The meat is packed and commonly covered with a layer of fat. Fat coverage reduces direct contact with air. It does not replace hygienic processing or appropriate storage. Example: pork rillettes packed under fat.

Q47

What is fermented fish sauce, and what happens to proteins during fermentation?

Fish sauce is a savoury liquid produced by fermenting fish with salt. Enzymes and microbial activity help break down proteins. Proteins form smaller peptides and amino acids. These compounds contribute to the characteristic savoury flavour. Example: traditional Southeast Asian fish sauce.

Q48

What is histamine, and why is its control important in fermented fish products?

Histamine is a biogenic amine that can form when certain bacteria convert histidine in fish. High levels can cause histamine food poisoning. Formation depends on raw-material quality, temperature and microbial activity. Proper handling and validated processing reduce risk. Example: temperature-abused fish may accumulate histamine.

Q49

List the major steps involved in traditional sausage making.

Select and prepare suitable meat and fat. Mince the meat and mix with salt, seasonings and suitable curing ingredients. Stuff the mixture into casings. Depending on the product, ferment, dry, smoke or cook it. Example: fermented sausages undergo controlled fermentation followed by drying.

Q50

Mention four factors that affect the safety and quality of traditional meat products.

Temperature affects microbial growth and processing rate. Moisture content and water activity influence stability. Salt concentration and fermentation conditions affect preservation. Hygiene, packaging and storage influence safety and quality. Example: properly dried and packaged meat is more stable than moist meat stored improperly.
