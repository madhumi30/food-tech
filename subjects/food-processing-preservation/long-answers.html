Q1. Explain the principle of food irradiation, the sources of radiation used, and its mechanism of food preservation. Discuss its applications, advantages, limitations, and safety aspects.

Answer based on the uploaded notes

Q1. Explain the principle of food irradiation, the sources of radiation used, and its mechanism of food preservation. Discuss its applications, advantages, limitations, and safety aspects. Food irradiation is the treatment of food with a specified dose of ionizing radiation. The uploaded lecture identifies gamma rays, electron beams and X-rays as sources, used in a radiation-shielded chamber. Radiation damages DNA or RNA directly and can also form free radicals when cellular water is radiolysed. These changes interfere with cell replication and can reduce or inactivate susceptible organisms. Applications listed in the notes include microbial decontamination of spices, pathogen reduction in meat and poultry, insect control and sprout inhibition. Advantages include a minimal temperature rise and treatment of refrigerated or frozen foods. Dose selection, regulation, hygiene and correct storage remain essential; irradiation does not replace them.

Core principles and supporting details

Definition and principle:Food irradiation treats food with a specified, controlled dose of ionizing radiation. Radiation damages microbial genetic material directly or indirectly, reducing the ability of target organisms to survive or reproduce. The lecture describes it as a volumetric treatment with minimal temperature rise.

Sources:(1) Gamma rays from radioisotope sources; (2) high-energy electron beams generated by an accelerator; (3) X-rays produced when accelerated electrons strike a target.

Applications:reducing pathogens and spoilage organisms in meat and poultry; disinfestation of grains and spices; inhibition of sprouting in potatoes and onions; and delaying ripening or deterioration in selected produce.

Advantages:treats a broad range of foods from dry spices to high-moisture foods; little temperature rise means refrigerated or frozen foods may be treated without thawing; irregular product shapes are less problematic than in some surface treatments; can reduce microbial hazards without adding chemical preservatives.

Limitations and controls:dose and conditions must be validated for the food and target; facilities are specialized and regulated; irradiation does not replace hygiene or suitable storage; unsuitable conditions can affect sensory/nutritional quality; cost and consumer acceptance can affect adoption.

Conclusion:Irradiation is a regulated, low-temperature-rise preservation technology used for microbial and insect control and shelf-life management. It must be part of a wider food-safety system.

Question-specific explanation / flowchart

The source lecture distinguishes the treatment purpose from the radiation source: the selected dose must match the target organism or insect and the food. A shielded treatment chamber and controlled handling are part of the process. The important exam point is that irradiation is a preservation step, not a substitute for sanitation, raw-material quality or suitable storage.

Q4. Define food concentration and explain its importance. Describe the major concentration methods with their principles, operation, applications, advantages and limitations.

Answer based on the uploaded notes

Q4. Define food concentration and explain its importance. Describe the major concentration methods with their principles, operation, applications, advantages and limitations. Food concentration means partial removal of moisture from liquid foods to increase total solids; the Unit 4 slides state that solids may be increased up to about 70%. It reduces product weight and volume and can reduce transport and storage costs. Liquid foods are often concentrated before drying because evaporators can remove early-stage water more economically than drying equipment. Common products include evaporated and sweetened condensed milk, fruit and vegetable juices, nectars, syrups, jams, jellies, tomato paste and fruit purées. Methods in the notes include solar concentration, open-kettle heating, flash evaporation, thin-film and vacuum evaporation, freeze concentration, ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis. The choice depends on the food and desired quality.

Core principles and supporting details

Definition:Concentration is partial moisture removal from liquid foods to increase total solids. The Unit 4 slides state values up to about 70%. It reduces mass and volume and is often done before drying because evaporators can remove early-stage water economically.

Importance:lowers transport and storage costs; creates convenient ingredients; reduces the water load for subsequent drying; increases soluble-solids and acid concentrations; and in sugar- or salt-rich foods, high osmotic pressure can inhibit microbial growth. Concentration alone does not guarantee safety for every food.

Methods:(1) Solar concentration—uses solar energy, e.g. salt recovery from seawater. (2) Open kettle—steam-heated vessel for some jams, jellies and soups. (3) Flash evaporation—rapid contact with a heating medium; the slides mention superheated steam around 150°C. (4) Thin-film evaporation—spreads food into a thin layer for rapid heat transfer. (5) Vacuum evaporation—reduces boiling temperature for heat-sensitive liquids. (6) Freeze concentration—freezes and separates water as ice, helping preserve juice aroma. (7) Membrane concentration—ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis separate components under pressure.

Applications:evaporated/condensed milk, fruit and vegetable juices, nectars, syrups, jams, jellies, tomato paste and fruit purées.

Conclusion:Selection depends on heat sensitivity, flavour retention, desired solids, energy use, membrane characteristics and cost.

Question-specific explanation / flowchart

For a method comparison, discuss the physical route used to remove water. Evaporation forms vapour by heating; freeze concentration separates some water as ice; membranes separate components under pressure. The Unit 4 material states that concentration can raise total solids to about 70%, but the appropriate endpoint is product-specific.

Q7. Explain ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis in food processing. Discuss their principles, membrane separation mechanisms, applications, advantages and limitations. Compare them.

Answer based on the uploaded notes

Q7. Explain ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis in food processing. Discuss their principles, membrane separation mechanisms, applications, advantages and limitations. Compare them. Ultrafiltration (UF) and reverse osmosis (RO) are pressure-driven membrane processes used for low-temperature separation and concentration. Liquid food is pumped against a perm-selective membrane; different components pass or remain according to membrane properties. The slides describe UF as retaining microscopic particles and many macromolecules while allowing water and some smaller solutes to pass, with pores around 0.01-0.1 micrometres. It is used in clarification of proteins, juices and dairy products. RO uses pressure to drive water through a selective membrane while retaining much of the dissolved material. Membrane permeability, rejection rates, material, useful life and cleaning affect performance. Unlike evaporation, membrane processing does not require boiling the water.

Core principles and supporting details

Principle:a liquid food is pressurized against a semipermeable membrane. Selected components pass through more readily than others. Unlike evaporation, membrane processes remove water without requiring a phase change.

Ultrafiltration:retains suspended particles and many macromolecules while water and some smaller solutes pass. The slides state pores around 0.01–0.1 μm and mention clarification of proteins, juices and dairy products.

Reverse osmosis:pressure drives water through a selective membrane while retaining much of the dissolved material, concentrating the food without boiling it.

Advantages:lower-temperature operation can protect heat-sensitive components; concentration/clarification may be achieved with lower thermal exposure; processes can be combined with evaporation or other unit operations.

Limitations:different foods require suitable membranes and operating conditions; fouling and cleaning matter; water permeability, solute rejection and membrane life determine performance. UF and RO perform different separations and are not interchangeable.

Conclusion:UF is associated with particle/macromolecule retention and clarification, while RO is commonly used to remove water while retaining dissolved constituents.

Reverse osmosis uses pressure to drive water through a selective membrane while retaining much of the dissolved material. Membrane fouling, permeability, rejection and cleaning influence operation.

Core principles and supporting details

Definition:Concentration is partial moisture removal from liquid foods to increase total solids. The Unit 4 slides state values up to about 70%. It reduces mass and volume and is often done before drying because evaporators can remove early-stage water economically.

Importance:lowers transport and storage costs; creates convenient ingredients; reduces the water load for subsequent drying; increases soluble-solids and acid concentrations; and in sugar- or salt-rich foods, high osmotic pressure can inhibit microbial growth. Concentration alone does not guarantee safety for every food.

Methods:(1) Solar concentration—uses solar energy, e.g. salt recovery from seawater. (2) Open kettle—steam-heated vessel for some jams, jellies and soups. (3) Flash evaporation—rapid contact with a heating medium; the slides mention superheated steam around 150°C. (4) Thin-film evaporation—spreads food into a thin layer for rapid heat transfer. (5) Vacuum evaporation—reduces boiling temperature for heat-sensitive liquids. (6) Freeze concentration—freezes and separates water as ice, helping preserve juice aroma. (7) Membrane concentration—ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis separate components under pressure.

Applications:evaporated/condensed milk, fruit and vegetable juices, nectars, syrups, jams, jellies, tomato paste and fruit purées.

Conclusion:Selection depends on heat sensitivity, flavour retention, desired solids, energy use, membrane characteristics and cost.

Core principles and supporting details

Purpose:controlled heat removes water and lowers aw, restricting microbial growth and slowing enzyme activity.

Advantages:longer shelf life when properly dried and packaged; lower transport/storage costs; convenient products and ingredients; production of coffee, raisins, pasta, flour, pulses, nuts, cereals, tea, spices, milk powder, egg powder, flavourings, colourings, enzymes and yeasts.

Q47. Draw a diagram showing conduction, convection and radiation in food processing. A labelled heat-transfer diagram should distinguish the three modes described in the uploaded notes: conduction through a solid food or equipment wall; convection between a surface and moving air, water or steam; and radiation as electromagnetic energy that does not require a material medium. The notes give Fourier s law for conduction, q = -kA(dT/dx), and the convection relation q = hA(Tfluid - Tsurface). Core principles and supporting details

Introduction:Heat transfer is essential in cooking, pasteurization, sterilization, evaporation, drying, freezing and thawing. The three modes often occur together.

Conduction:heat travels through material without bulk movement. It matters inside solid foods and through metal equipment. Fourier’s law is q = −kA(dT/dx), where k is thermal conductivity and A is area. Heat moves from higher to lower temperature. Example: heat penetrating the centre of a solid food.

Convection:heat transfer between a surface and a moving fluid such as air, water or steam. It can be natural or forced. A common expression is q = hA(Tfluid − Tsurface), where h is the convective coefficient. Example: hot-air drying or heating food in boiling water.

Radiation:energy travels by electromagnetic waves and requires no material medium. It is important in high-temperature applications such as grilling and baking. Emission depends on surface temperature, area and emissivity; net exchange also depends on surrounding temperature.

Comparison:conduction occurs through a material; convection occurs between a surface and moving fluid; radiation is electromagnetic transfer. During hot-air drying, convection heats the surface and conduction moves heat into the food.

Conclusion:identifying the dominant mode helps design safe, efficient food-processing equipment and estimate processing time.
