Q1. Explain the principle of food irradiation, the sources of radiation used, and its mechanism of food preservation. Discuss its applications, advantages, limitations, and safety aspects.
Introduction Food irradiation exposes food to a controlled dose of ionizing radiation to improve safety or storage life. Principle and sources Ionizing radiation transfers energy that can damage the DNA of susceptible microorganisms and insects. The main sources are gamma rays, machine-generated electron beams and X-rays. Dose is measured in gray (Gy) or kilogray (kGy). Mechanism Radiation can act directly on microbial genetic material or indirectly through reactive species formed from water. Damage can prevent microorganisms from multiplying. The effectiveness depends on dose, organism resistance, food composition and processing conditions. Applications • Reducing selected pathogens in meat and poultry. • Reducing microbial contamination in spices. • Controlling insects in cereals, pulses and selected produce. • Inhibiting sprouting in potatoes and onions. • Treating certain products for quarantine purposes. Advantages Irradiation can reduce microbial hazards, extend shelf life and treat some foods with little temperature rise. Suitable packaged foods may be treated without reopening the package. Limitations and safety It cannot restore spoiled food or remove every toxin or chemical contaminant. Depending on the food and dose, flavour, texture or some vitamins may change. Approved irradiation does not make food radioactive. The treatment must comply with applicable regulations and validated dose requirements. Conclusion and example Food irradiation is a useful tool alongside hygiene and correct storage. Example: spice irradiation reduces microbial contamination, while potato irradiation can inhibit sprouting.
Q2. Describe the applications of food irradiation in food preservation. Explain how irradiation controls microorganisms, insects, sprouting, and food spoilage.
Introduction Food irradiation can be applied to selected foods to reduce microbial hazards, control insects, inhibit sprouting and extend shelf life. Microbial control Ionizing radiation damages microbial DNA and other cellular components. Susceptible organisms lose their ability to reproduce. Applications include selected pathogen reduction in meat and poultry and microbial-load reduction in spices. Effectiveness depends on the target organism, dose and food composition. Insect disinfestation Insects damage stored cereals, pulses, nuts and produce. Irradiation can disrupt development or reproduction, depending on the treatment. It may also support quarantine requirements for selected commodities. Sprout inhibition Irradiation can inhibit sprouting in crops such as potatoes and onions, helping maintain storage quality and market value. Parasite control Appropriate validated irradiation treatments can inactivate certain parasites in selected meat and fish products. The required treatment depends on the target parasite and food. Shelf-life extension Reducing susceptible spoilage organisms may delay deterioration. Packaging, temperature and initial quality remain important after treatment. Advantages and limitations The technology can treat suitable foods with little temperature rise and may reduce reliance on some fumigants. However, it does not eliminate every hazard, correct poor-quality raw materials or replace refrigeration and hygiene where needed. Conclusion and example Food irradiation is useful when selected and validated for a specific product. Example: irradiation can reduce microbial contamination in spices and inhibit sprouting in potatoes.
Q3. Compare food irradiation with conventional thermal processing in terms of principle, effects on food quality, microbial control, advantages and limitations.
Introduction Food irradiation and thermal processing are preservation methods that differ in how energy is delivered and how microorganisms are inactivated. Principles Thermal processing uses heat for a specified time and temperature to damage essential microbial structures. Examples include pasteurization and sterilization. Irradiation exposes food to a controlled ionizing-radiation dose, which damages genetic material and other cellular components. Quality effects Heat can soften texture, alter colour and flavour, and reduce heat-sensitive vitamins. Irradiation often causes little temperature rise, but may still change flavour, texture or nutrient content depending on the food and dose. Microbial control Thermal-process effectiveness depends on time, temperature, food acidity, heat penetration and microbial heat resistance. Irradiation effectiveness depends on dose, target-organism resistance and food composition. Both require validation and hygienic handling. Advantages Thermal processing is well established and can provide effective microbial control. Irradiation can treat selected foods with little heating and can control insects or sprouting in suitable products. Limitations Thermal processing can damage heat-sensitive quality attributes. Irradiation is not suitable for every food or hazard and requires specialized equipment and regulatory compliance. Neither method can make spoiled food acceptable. Conclusion The methods are complementary. Selection depends on product characteristics, hazards, desired shelf life, quality requirements and cost. Example: pasteurization is used for milk, while irradiation may be used to reduce microbial contamination in spices.
